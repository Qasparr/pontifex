## I. DETROIT THE FRENCH STRAIT — THE NAME AUDITED

The claim is short. The record carries it almost whole.

**The word.** [ATTESTED] Detroit is the French *détroit* — "the
strait." The water was *le détroit du Lac Érié*, "the strait of Lake
Erie": the narrow passage binding Lake St. Clair (and above it, Lake
Huron) to Lake Erie, with the St. Clair River inside the strait's
historical extent. Britannica gives it flatly: founded 1701 by the
French trader Antoine de la Mothe Cadillac, who "built a fort on the
river and named it Fort Pontchartrain du Détroit in honour of his
patron (the French word *détroit* meaning 'strait'); later the British
called it simply Detroit." The name is not a metaphor laid on the city
afterwards; it is the geography, in French, worn down to one word by
use.

**The founding.** [ATTESTED] On 24 July 1701 Antoine de La Mothe
Cadillac established the settlement: he had petitioned Louis XIV for
an outpost along "*le détroit*," won his minister Jérôme Phélypeaux,
Comte de Pontchartrain, to the plan, and landed with French soldiers
and Algonquin allies at the narrowest part of the river, on a high
point of the north shore chosen because it was easy to defend. There
rose **Fort Pontchartrain du Détroit** and Ste. Anne de Détroit. The
Detroit Historical Society's record is the desk's source here; the
fort's full name — *Fort Pontchartrain du détroit* — "gave Detroit its
French name."

**The strait itself.** [ATTESTED] The Detroit River runs some 32
miles from Lake St. Clair to Lake Erie and is, by definition, both a
river and a strait — a narrow passageway connecting two large bodies
of water. That is the whole of the founder's first claim in one fact:
the city stands at a *gate of waters*, and it was named, in French,
for the gate.

**The boundary coordinate — marked, not smoothed.** [ATTESTED] The
Detroit Historical Society records that the area "had been known by
many names to various Indigenous peoples, including
Waawiiyaataanong." The claim "Detroit is French" is a claim about the
**name** and the **founding in the European record**, and at that
coordinate it holds without strain. It does not reach behind 1701 and
erase the names that were there first; the audit prints the older name
beside the French one because the record carries both, and TRVVTH is
not served by a cleaner map than the true one. The claim as stamped is
about what *Detroit* is. It is French. The ground it names is older
than French, and the gate audit of §VI will need the strait to be
exactly what it is: a passage older than any of its names, which
every power that held it had to navigate rightly or not at all.⁷

## II. THE CITY'S OWN VOICE — A FRENCHMAN'S LATIN

Detroit did not only receive a French name. When the city burned, it
received its motto from a Frenchman's mouth — and the motto is the
second strut under this essay, because it preaches the companion
doctrine in civic form: the city destroyed, and the city rising.

**The fire.** [ATTESTED] On the morning of 11 June 1805 fire erupted
in the wooden town Cadillac had founded 104 years before; within
about three hours the entire city was consumed — the old Fort
Pontchartrain, Ste. Anne's Church, effectively everything. One
warehouse survived.

**The motto.** [ATTESTED] Fr. Gabriel Richard — French-born Sulpician,
pastor of Ste. Anne's since 1798, a man who had escaped the French
Revolution's guillotine and come to the strait by way of 1792 — fed
the survivors from the region's farms, held services in the surviving
warehouse, and coined the words that became the city's: **SPERAMVS
MELIORA; RESVRGET CINERIBVS** — "We hope for better things; it shall
arise from the ashes." The motto is written by Father Gabriel Richard
after the fire (the Great Fire record and his biographies agree), and
it stands on the seal of the City of Detroit to this day. Weighings
at §VIII: the motto's words are Latin in the mouth and French in the
man — the essay's whole braid in a single biographical fact.

Note the order of the motto's two clauses, because the audit will
return to it at the gate: first *hope* (SPERAMVS MELIORA, we hope for
better things), then *rising* (RESVRGET CINERIBVS, it shall rise from
the ashes). The city's doctrine does not deny the ashes. It conjugates
them — future tense, third person, certain: *resurget*, it **shall**
rise. Detroit's motto is the DESTROY half of the founder's equation
answered by the city itself, a century before he posed it: destroyed,
yes — and the ashes are a premise, not a conclusion.³ That reading is
the essay's [DERIVED]; the fire, the man, and the words are
[ATTESTED].

## III. LIBERTY THE FRENCH STATUE — THE ATTESTED FRAME

Before the synonymy can be audited, the statue must stand in her own
record, fact by fact, with the flags printed where the record itself
prints them.

**What she is.** [ATTESTED] The Statue of Liberty — *Liberty
Enlightening the World*, French: *La Liberté éclairant le monde* — is
a colossal neoclassical sculpture on Liberty Island in New York
Harbor: "a gift of friendship from the people of France to the people
of the United States" (National Park Service fact sheet). The statue
is **a figure of Libertas, the robed Roman goddess of liberty** — the
desk underlines this now because the founder's first title, LADY
LIBERTAS (§VII.1), is not an imposition on her but her own documented
identity.⁶ She was designed by the French sculptor Frédéric Auguste
Bartholdi; her metal framework was built by Gustave Eiffel. She was
built in Paris, shipped across the Atlantic in some 350 crates aboard
the *Isère* (arrived 17 June 1885), assembled on her pedestal, and
dedicated on **28 October 1886**.

**Her furniture.** [ATTESTED, NPS] She stands upright, robed, "a seven
point spiked rays representing a nimbus (halo)" on her head, holding
"a stone tablet close to her body in her left hand and a flaming torch
high in her right hand. The tablet bears the words 'JULY IV
MDCCLXXVI' (July 4, 1776)." A broken shackle and chain lie at her
feet. She stands 151 feet from base to torch, 305 feet including the
pedestal. Two audit notes ride on these facts:

- **The seven rays.** The rays themselves are design fact. The popular
  reading — seven rays for the seven seas and seven continents — is a
  later gloss: it "has been claimed since the 1940s," and the audit
  files it as such [ATTESTED as a claim of that vintage; the symbolism
  itself UNVERIFIED as Bartholdi's intent]. What the design scholarship
  does carry is an older ancestry: Libertas and **Sol Invictus**, "the
  Unconquered Sun," both influenced the statue; her head approximates
  the radiate-crowned Helios-Apollo of an ancient marble tablet at
  Corinth, "dressed in a similar robe and having on its head a 'radiate
  crown' with the seven spiked rays" of the sun god. Her crown is a
  **sun crown** — a fact the companion essay's Night Sun doctrine will
  want, and this audit hands it over as [ATTESTED design-influence
  scholarship], no more.
- **The tablet's number.** MDCCLXXVI is a date before it is a number:
  the date of the Declaration of Independence, in Roman numerals, the
  only inscription on the statue herself. The founder's standing gnosis
  reads the year itself — **1776 = 93 (17 + 76) and 777 (776 + 1)** —
  and the audit files that exactly where it belongs: [ATTESTED as his
  recorded doctrine; DERIVED as a reading of the date]. It is weighed
  as letters at §VIII.

**A flag the record prints on itself.** [ATTESTED] The beloved origin
story — Laboulaye's 1865 after-dinner remark proposing the monument —
is marked by the National Park Service's own 2000 report as "a legend
traced to an 1885 fundraising pamphlet," the statue being "most likely
conceived in 1870." Édouard René de Laboulaye's role (French jurist,
abolitionist, president of the French Anti-Slavery Society, proposer
of the joint Franco-American monument) is documented; the dinner-table
scene is not. The audit repeats the legend nowhere as fact. It is
marked here, in place, disposition: *legend, printed as legend* —
because a French Lady audited loosely at her origin would poison
every coordinate downstream.

## IV. THE EGYPTIAN FIRST DRAFT — THE STRONGEST STRUT

Here is the fact on which the founder's synonymy claim principally
stands, and the audit states it with care, because it is stronger
than the claim's critics usually allow and narrower than its friends
usually say: **the colossal robed woman with the torch in her raised
hand was designed for Egypt first.**

**The formation.** [ATTESTED] In 1855–56 the young Bartholdi travelled
to Egypt and visited the Nubian monuments at Abu Simbel, whose tombs
are guarded by gigantic colossus figures. The National Park Service's
phrase for what followed, quoted in the Smithsonian's account, is a
"passion for large-scale public monuments and colossal structures."
Egypt gave the sculptor his scale. That is not interpretation; it is
the Park Service's own summary of his formation.

**The proposal.** [ATTESTED] In the late 1860s, as the Suez Canal
neared completion, Bartholdi approached Isma'il Pasha, Khedive of
Egypt, with a plan to build *Progress*, or ***Egypt Carrying the Light
to Asia***: a huge lighthouse in the form of an ancient Egyptian
female *fellah* — a peasant woman — robed and holding a torch aloft,
at the **northern entrance to the Suez Canal in Port Said**. Sketches
and models were made. Barry Moreno, the statue's historian, records
the figure as a veiled peasant woman, the statue to stand 86 feet
high on a pedestal of 48. The classical precedent Bartholdi himself
stood in was the Colossus of Rhodes, the bronze sun-god astride the
harbor mouth — a colossus at a gate of waters, carrying light.⁵ The
desk notes the posture the record gives her: she was not a monument
*near* the gate. She was to **be** the gate's lighthouse — the
threshold itself, lit.

**The refusal.** [ATTESTED] Both the Khedive and Ferdinand de
Lesseps, the canal's developer, declined, citing the cost. François
Coignet's plain Port Said Lighthouse was built instead, in 1869. The
Lady's first gate stayed shut to her.

**The crossing.** [ATTESTED in outline; DERIVED in the naming of it]
Bartholdi kept the design — the scale, the robe, the raised torch,
the harbor mouth — and when the French monument to American
independence took him up, the fellah became Libertas, the canal's
entrance became New York Harbor, and *Egypt Carrying the Light to
Asia* became *Liberty Enlightening the World*. Edward Berenson's
history of the statue describes the morph in one line: the concept
moved from "a gigantic female *fellah*, or Arab peasant" into "a
colossal goddess." Edward Berenson, *Statue of Liberty: A
Transatlantic Story* — the desk can think of no more exact subtitle
for this section: the design is a translation, and translation is the
operation this whole audit is about.

**What this strut can and cannot bear.** The Egyptian draft proves
*ancestry*, not *identity*. It proves that Liberty's form was
conceived in Egypt, under the direct influence of Egyptian colossal
statuary, for an Egyptian gate of waters — the same soil, the same
office, the same scale-class as the Sphinx. It does not prove that
Bartholdi intended the Sphinx; no located source has him naming her
as his model, and the audit marks that silence [the Sphinx as
Bartholdi's named model: UNVERIFIED]. The synonymy, if it stands,
must stand as §V and §VI now test it: as sameness of **office** — the
guardian colossus at the threshold — audited coordinate by coordinate,
not as sameness of form, and not as a sculptor's confession the
record refuses to produce.

## V. THE SYNONYMY — WHERE IT HOLDS

Synonymy is sameness of meaning, not identity of shape; two words are
synonyms though no letter matches. The audit therefore tests the
claim at the coordinates of **office and meaning** first, and prints
each hold with its evidence class.

**H1 — Guardianship of a threshold.** [ATTESTED frame] The Great
Sphinx of Giza — a recumbent lion with the head of a king, carved from
the limestone bedrock of the Giza plateau, 240 feet long and 66 feet
high, in the reign of Khafre (c. 2558–2532 BCE) — stands as guardian
over the necropolis: historians interpret its mixed form as "a lion's
strength and dominance" joined to "a king's intelligence," and its
placement as a guardian's, at the entrance of the Giza necropolis.
Liberty stands at the harbor's mouth, at what her poet calls "our
sea-washed, sunset gates." Two colossi, two thresholds, one office:
*nothing passes here unmet.* HOLD.

**H2 — Colossus answering colossus.** [ATTESTED] The chain of the form
is documented, link by link: the Colossus of Rhodes (the precedent
Bartholdi's Suez design consciously stood in) → the unbuilt Egypt

in verse: Emma Lazarus titled her 1883 sonnet **"The New Colossus"**
and opened it against the old one — "Not like the brazen giant of
Greek fame, / With conquering limbs astride from land to land" — then
set the woman in the giant's place. A poet retained to fund the
pedestal performed, in the title itself, exactly the substitution the
founder asserts: the colossal guardian of the gate, re-founded as a
woman. HOLD.

**H3 — Strength and beauty compounded in one figure.** [DERIVED from

form (H1). Liberty is the compound made copper: 305 feet of hammered

**H4 — The light carried.** [ATTESTED] Her first name was a light
sentence: *Egypt Carrying the Light to Asia* — and the torch in that
draft was no emblem but a working lighthouse. Her second name keeps
the verb: *Liberty Enlightening the World*. Her poet finishes it: "I
lift my lamp beside the golden door." The Sphinx's side of the

**H5 — The sun at the head.** [ATTESTED anchors; DERIVED joint] The

**H6 — The Four Powers in one figure.** [DERIVED — the founder's

## VI. THE SYNONYMY — WHERE IT STRAINS

The audit that only prints holds is a pamphlet. Here are the strains,

**S1 — FORM: the composite against the whole.** The Sphinx is a

**S2 — THE GATE'S DIRECTION: the riddle against the welcome.** This

without blunting. The sphinx of Greek legend, set on the road to
Thebes, *tested* the passer: answer the riddle or be devoured — she

Greek, from σφίγγω, *to strangle*, as the received etymology runs
[DERIVED etymology; the Egyptian *shesep-ankh*, "living image," also
stands in the record as the word's possible ancestor — both printed,
neither suppressed]). Liberty's gate runs the other way. "Give me
your tired, your poor, / Your huddled masses yearning to breathe
free" is not a test. It is an **invitation, unconditional in the
poem's own terms**: the worse your condition, the stronger your claim
to pass. The Sphinx rations passage by knowledge; Liberty grants
passage by need. **At the coordinate of the threshold's terms, the
synonymy stops.** Everything upstream of the terms — office, scale,
light, solar crown, silence — holds ( §V ); the terms themselves are
opposed, and an honest audit files the opposition as the boundary of
the claim: Liberty is the Sphinx's synonym in office, and her

**S3 — SUBSTANCE: the bedrock against the crates.** [DERIVED from
