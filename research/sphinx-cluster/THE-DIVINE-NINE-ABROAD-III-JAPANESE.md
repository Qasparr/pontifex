## II. THE FIVE PURE SOUNDS — THE DIALECT'S OWN CANON

Japanese sounds five pure vowels, and it sounds them in a ruled
order that is itself a canon: **あ い う え お** — *a i u e o* —
the head row of the gojūon, the "fifty-sound" table that orders
the whole syllabary. Each with its reasoning — why this sound, at
this seat, in this tongue:

| Seat | Kana | Sound | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | あ | a | The open vowel; the table's first cell and the order's head, as Α heads the Greek ladder. |
| 2 | い | i | The high front vowel; the seat Tokyo will learn to whisper (§III.4). |
| 3 | う | u | The high back vowel — but unrounded [ɯ], lips unpursed: the dialect's own u, not Europe's. |
| 4 | え | e | The mid front vowel; half of the collapsing pair (§III.3). |
| 5 | お | o | The mid back rounded vowel; the seat that lengthens by two different brothers (§III.2). |

[The five phonemes and the order ATTESTED — the standard phonology
of Japanese: /a i u e o/, the u unrounded. The gojūon order
descends from the Siddham (bonji) phonetic science carried into
Japan with Buddhism, which arranged the sounds on the Sanskrit
phoneticians' grid of place and manner; the fifty-sound table's
systematisation is credited by tradition to the Heian monk
Myōgaku. The desk cites the transmission at this level and no
finer.]

## III. THE HYBRID MACHINERY — FOUR MECHANISMS, WRITTEN IN THE OPEN

The Greek hybrid seats are letters: ει and ου, two signs sounded
as one long syllable, hybrids *by taxonomy*. Japanese has no
improper diphthongs to seat. What it has is machinery — four
operations by which its sounds fuse, extend, collapse, and
withdraw — and its script confesses each operation on its face.
The four, each with its evidence and its seat-coordinate:

### III.1 — Yōon: the small letter (fusion within one beat)

The palatalised syllables — きゃ *kya*, しゃ *sha*, ちゃ *cha*,
じゃ *ja* — are written with two kana: the consonant's kana at
full size and the glide's kana **written small**: ゃ ゅ ょ. The
small kana even carry the mechanism's confession in their house
name — *sutegana*, "thrown-away letters," signs written reduced
because their sound is not sounded whole. Two signs occupy one
mora: the consonant seat and the y-glide fuse into a single beat
that is neither the consonant's plain syllable nor two syllables.
This is the Greek diphthong's exact structural cousin, with the
polarity reversed: Greek writes two letters *full-size* and
sounds one syllable; Japanese writes the second letter *small*
and sounds one beat — and prints the reduction where any eye can
see it. The hybrid abroad wears its hybridity in its letterforms.
[The mechanism ATTESTED — standard kana orthography and mora
phonology. The small letter is the audit's first exhibit for
Rule 2 (§VI).]

### III.2 — The long vowel: the offset made audible (extension across two beats)

A Japanese long vowel is one quality stretched over two morae —
and the spelling *declares the constant*: おう and おお both
sound *ō*, えい and ええ both sound (in the lengthened reading)
*ē*. The second kana is not a second vowel; it is the length
itself, written. Read with the home doctrine's eyes, this is the
**OFFSET** in phonetic dress: the pure vowel stands true at its
coordinate (お, one mora of o), and beside it stands a constant,
declared, admitted quantity — +1 mora — that denies neither the
vowel nor itself. Black's offset is a counterbalancing sum
admitted beside a total; the chōon is a counterbalancing beat
admitted beside a vowel, and the admission is printed in the
word. The desk knows no purer exhibit of the second relation in
any dialect: an offset that *spells itself*. [The phonology
ATTESTED — two morae, one quality; the reading as OFFSET is the
audit's structural classification, DERIVED under the founder's
doctrine, and the Gate rules it at that tier (§X).]

### III.3 — EI: the reverse hybrid (collapse across two beats)

The spelling えい (*ei*) is, in ordinary Tokyo pronunciation,
sounded **ē** — one long pure vowel. Careful diction and song
restore the glide [ei]; the street collapses it. Set it beside
III.1 and the pair completes the dialect's confession: yōon is
two signs fused inside one beat; *ei* is two beats' signs
collapsed into one quality. A written diphthong sounded as a
monophthong — the Greek improper diphthong inverted: ει is two
letters sounded as one *syllable by the grammarians' rule*, while
えい is two morae sounded as one *quality by the dialect's
practice*, the glide worn away in the mouth rather than in the
taxonomy. Its zero point prints exactly: (sign = second kana of
えい; seam = spelling against sound; the seat is pure by sound,
hybrid by dress). Any audit that classifies えい by its letters
alone will seat a hybrid where the dialect sounds a pure vowel —
the type case for Rule 4. [The pronunciation ATTESTED — standard
Tokyo phonology, [eː] in ordinary speech, [ei] in careful
registers.]

### III.4 — The devoiced vowel: the ghost seat (voice withdrawn, seat kept)

In Tokyo speech the high vowels い and う are **devoiced**
between voiceless consonants and after a voiceless consonant at
the pause: すき *suki* sounds [sɯ̥ki], です *desu* sounds
[desɯ̥] — the vowel articulated, timed, and *whispered*, its
voicing withdrawn while its mora still counts in the rhythm.
The kana stands at full size. The beat is kept. The voice is
gone. Here is the founder's characterisation of his hybrid seats
— *fluid, both at once* — in its most literal phonetic form this
essay will find in any tongue: a vowel present and absent in the
same instant, seated and silent, neither sounded nor removed.
The Greek seats are fluid by taxonomy; the Tokyo ghost is fluid
by articulation. [The devoicing ATTESTED — the textbook
description of Tokyo Japanese. The seat-reading DERIVED.]

### III.5 — The lost seats: ゐ and ゑ (the dialect's own digamma)

Until the script reform of 1946 the kana table carried two more
vowel seats: ゐ (*wi*) and ゑ (*we*). Their sounds had merged
into い and え centuries before the letters were retired — the
seats outlived their sounds, exactly as the Greek ϝ (digamma)
outlived its /w/ in the numeral system, surviving as ϛ (stigma),
the figure 6, a ghost that still closes the founder's mirror
proof. In 1946 the modern kana usage (gendai kanazukai, Cabinet
Notification of that year) struck ゐ and ゑ from the living
table; they stand in old texts, in names, and in this essay —
marked, not vanished, in the dialect's own history as in the
house's. One more seat of the same family: を (*wo*), retained
in the modern table but sounded *o*, its seat kept as the
grammar's particle while its sound merged into お's — a seat
kept, a sound surrendered, the inverse distribution of III.4's
ghost. [The history ATTESTED — the 1946 reform and the earlier
mergers are documentary. The digamma parallel DERIVED.]

## VI. THE NINE RULES FOR HYBRID DIALECTS — AS JAPANESE EXHIBITS THEM

Derived from the evidence of §§II–V, stated as rules of reading
for any dialect the house audits after this one. Each rule
carries its exhibit and its tier; the set is the founder's by

**Rule 1 — Weigh the sound; seat the sign afterwards.**
A seat is assigned by the sound's behaviour in the dialect;
spelling is evidence for the seat, never the seat itself.
*Exhibit:* えい, hybrid by dress, pure by sound (§III.3).
[Tier: doctrine DERIVED from the audit.]

**Rule 2 — The small-letter rule.**
Where a dialect writes a letter reduced, a hybrid sits at that
seat: reduction of form is the script's confession of fusion.
*Exhibit:* the sutegana ゃ ゅ ょ of the yōon (§III.1) — the only
mechanism in this essay that prints its hybridity in the
letterform itself. [Exhibit ATTESTED; rule DERIVED.]

**Rule 3 — Length is an offset, never a seat.**
A doubled vowel is the pure vowel plus a constant, declared in
the spelling; crown the vowel, declare the constant, and seat
no "long vowel" as a new pure sound. *Exhibit:* おう/おお = ō,
the offset made audible (§III.2). [DERIVED, under the founder's
Offset Law.]

**Rule 4 — Classify by the sound; print the seam.**
Where letters and sound disagree, the classification follows
the sound and the disagreement is printed with its coordinates
— sign, seam, direction — never silently harmonised.
*Exhibit:* the collapse of えい, zero point at its second
kana (§III.3). [DERIVED, under the Zero-Point discipline.]

**Rule 5 — The ghost rule.**
A sound whose voice is withdrawn while its timing stands
occupies its seat still: present-absent is a state of a seat,
not a vacancy in it. *Exhibit:* the devoiced い/う of Tokyo
(§III.4) — and its mirror-kin abroad, を, the seat kept with

**Rule 6 — Order before number.**
A dialect is first weighed in its own ruled order; foreign

never retro-fitted into the canon. *Exhibit:* the gojūon

**Rule 7 — One work, many dresses.**
