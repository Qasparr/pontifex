## II. THE GERMAN INVENTORY

German writes its vowels with five pure letters, three bent letters,
and a row of pairs:

**The pure five: A E I O U.** Five letters for a vowel space richer
than five sounds — German distinguishes long and short at every
seat (a/aa, e/eh, and the doubling and Dehnungs-h spellings), but the
*letters* are five, and it is letters this audit weighs. [ATTESTED.]

**The bent three: Ä Ö Ü — the umlauts.** An umlaut is a vowel bent
by another vowel's ghost. The mechanism is historical and exact: in
the Old High German period a stem vowel was mutated — raised and
fronted — by an *i* or *j* standing in the following syllable
(*i-mutation*). The conditioning vowel then weakened and, in word
after word, vanished from speech altogether; the bend it caused
remained. The plural of *Gast* is *Gäste*; of *Haus*, *Häuser*; of
*Fuß*, *Füße* — the ghost survives in the ending's e, or nowhere but
the bend itself. Written first as digraphs (ae, oe, ue), the second
letter was later set *above* the first (aͤ, oͤ, uͤ), and in the
German hand (Kurrent) that little *e* is two short strokes — so
when print shrank the seated e to a mark, the mark was **two dots**:
the umlaut sign is a letter reduced to its strokes, a vowel's ghost
kept as punctuation. Jacob Grimm, who systematised the phenomenon in
the *Deutsche Grammatik*, gave it the name *Umlaut* itself.
[ATTESTED — historical phonology and paleography at this level.]

The essay's title for the three, therefore, is exact, not
decorative: the umlauts are **hybrids by birth** — one letter
carrying two sounds' history, the stem vowel and the vanished guest
that bent it. Where the Greek hybrids are two letters sounding one
sound, the German umlaut is the reciprocal figure: **one letter,
sounded once, fathered by two.**

**The gliding row: the diphthongs.** EI (also spelled AI), AU,
EU (also spelled ÄU), and IE. Of these, the first three are true
glides — one syllable moving between two vowel positions. IE is
not: in native words *ie* denotes the single long sound [iː]
(*Liebe*, *sie*), two letters in one sound's dress — which is, to
the letter, the grammarians' definition of the Greek **improper
diphthong** (§V). And the row holds one more bend: **ÄU is AU
umlauted** (*Haus → Häuser*) — the i-mutation applied to a diphthong,
a hybrid of a hybrid. [ATTESTED phonetics.]
