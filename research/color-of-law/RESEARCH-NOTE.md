## 2. "COLOR OF LAW" — THE DOCTRINE

**The core idea.** "Under color of law" means acting with the *appearance or semblance of legal authority* — misusing power that the actor holds only because the state gave it to them. The critical point most people miss: **it expands liability, it doesn't excuse it.** An official who abuses state power can't dodge suit by saying "that wasn't authorized, so it wasn't state action." The doctrine says the abuse *is* state action, so the victim can sue.

**The statute — 42 U.S.C. § 1983** (Civil Rights Act of 1871, originally the Ku Klux Klan Act). It makes liable "[e]very person who, under color of any statute, ordinance, regulation, custom, or usage, of any State or Territory or the District of Columbia, subjects, or causes to be subjected, any citizen... to the deprivation of any rights, privileges, or immunities secured by the Constitution and laws."

Two elements a plaintiff must prove:
1. The defendant acted **under color of state law**; and
2. The conduct deprived the plaintiff of a **federal constitutional or statutory right**.

**Landmark cases (real citations):**
- *United States v. Classic*, 313 U.S. 299 (1941) — the definition: "Misuse of power, possessed by virtue of state law and made possible only because the wrongdoer is clothed with the authority of state law, is action taken 'under color of' state law."
- *Screws v. United States*, 325 U.S. 91 (1945) — criminal counterpart (18 U.S.C. § 242). A Georgia sheriff beat a handcuffed Black prisoner to death; the Court held he acted under color of law even though state law forbade the beating. Added a specific-intent ("willful") requirement for the criminal statute.
- *Monroe v. Pape*, 365 U.S. 167 (1961) — Chicago police ransacked a home without a warrant and detained the family; held they acted under color of Illinois law despite violating it. The modern § 1983 workhorse case.
- *Monell v. Dep't of Soc. Servs.*, 436 U.S. 658 (1978) — municipalities are "persons" under § 1983 (partially overruling *Monroe* on that point), but no respondeat superior: the city is liable only for its own policy or custom.
- *Dennis v. Sparks*, 449 U.S. 24 (1980) — **private parties** who corruptly conspire with a state actor (there, a judge) act under color of law.
- *Lugar v. Edmondson Oil Co.*, 462 U.S. 922 (1982) — two-part test for when a private party counts as a state actor: (1) the deprivation results from a right/privilege created by the state, and (2) the party can fairly be said to be a state actor (joint participation).
- *West v. Atkins*, 487 U.S. 42 (1988) — a private doctor under contract with the state to treat prisoners acts under color of law.
- *Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971) — the federal counterpart: implied damages action against *federal* officers (§ 1983 covers only state actors).
- *Harlow v. Fitzgerald*, 457 U.S. 800 (1982) — **qualified immunity**: officials are shielded unless they violated "clearly established" law. The main defense that defeats § 1983 suits in practice.

**Criminal side:** 18 U.S.C. § 242 — federal crime of depriving rights "under color of law" (DOJ prosecutes; the *Screws* statute).

**Labeling the colloquial/sovereign-citizen usage:** In sov-cit filings and videos, "color of law" is often invoked to mean *fake or illegitimate* law — i.e., officials enforcing statutes are "acting under color of law" as pirates with no real authority. **That is not the legal doctrine.** The doctrine does the reverse: it treats even *unauthorized* official acts as state action *so that victims have a remedy*. Anyone citing "color of law" to void an official's authority has the doctrine backwards.

**Practical note for Johnathan's Samsung matter:** § 1983 requires *state action*. Samsung is a private corporation; color-of-law doctrine would not reach it unless a state actor were jointly involved (the *Dennis*/*Lugar* conspiracy route — a high bar). His demand notice sits in state consumer-protection / common-law territory, not § 1983 territory. Counsel's lane for the rest.
